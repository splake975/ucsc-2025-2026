Version C: Combined statement (use when one essay must cover both)
Many of the first algorithms we learn, such as those for longest increasing subsequence and edit distance, are dynamic programs: they fill in a table of answers to smaller subproblems until the final answer appears. This is already a striking trade, since it often takes a question whose search space is exponentially large and answers it in polynomial time. My current project asks when we can do even better: what can we conclude about a problem if we do not allow ourselves to fill in that whole table? I did not start out in computer science, and the way I got here is part of why I am sure about this direction.
At UC Davis I earned a bachelor's degree in mathematics and physics with high honors. My undergraduate thesis, advised by Martin Fraas, became a paper in the Journal of Mathematical Physics, "Poincaré disk as a model of squeezed states of a harmonic oscillator," which describes a correspondence between squeezed states of a quantum harmonic oscillator and the geometric motions on the hyperbolic disk. He suggested that such a correspondence might exist; I proved it, and showed that it gives a way to visualize questions in control theory. That project taught me to look for the picture that makes a complicated family of objects behave simply.
My first real exposure to theoretical computer science came from my roommate, who was doing research on automata and regular expressions. He would describe machines that read a string one symbol at a time and the sharp limits on what they can recognize, and eventually I started trying to help him think through the problems he was stuck on. It was the same pleasure I had found in mathematics and physics: a precise question and an argument that settles it. I came to UC Santa Cruz for a master's degree in computer science to find out whether this new interest would last. In my first quarter I took classes from three theory professors and found that theoretical computer science was the kind of mathematics I had been looking for.
I began working with C. Seshadhri on estimating the longest increasing subsequence of a long sequence, which is the same as measuring how far the sequence is from sorted, while reading only a tiny fraction of it. Longest increasing subsequence is one of the dynamic programs I mentioned at the start: solving it exactly means filling in a table, and the question is how much of that table we can avoid. Saks and Seshadhri showed this can be done in time polylogarithmic in the length of the sequence. My project is to rebuild their result into a simpler form: I have reworked two of its central arguments, a safety condition and a dichotomy argument, with the goal of a multiplicative guarantee, meaning that for any accuracy I choose the estimate is within that factor of the true distance. Because longest increasing subsequence is a dynamic program, this result points to a broader question, and that is how we arrived at our next step. Professor Seshadhri and I are now pursuing the question of what properties a dynamic programming problem needs so that it can be approximated top down, as the longest increasing subsequence algorithm is. A companion question is where the approach fails, since an answer to "why not" is a statement about the limits of approximation and so connects to complexity theory. Beyond this project, I want my PhD to follow the question of how much work a problem really requires wherever it leads in algorithms and complexity, including randomized and sublinear algorithms.
I have presented the simplified algorithm at the UC Santa Cruz theory seminar, which I am hosting this quarter, to an audience working across theory. I have also tutored undergraduate algorithms and served as a teaching assistant for undergraduate discrete mathematics. In teaching I like to push students into the areas where they are unclear, such as empty sets and the difference between "element of" and "subset," edge cases that rarely appear in standard exercises.

Most people have their times tables memorized before they turn ten. I learned only a few products, such as the squares, and worked the rest out from them. I still work this way: I decide what is worth remembering, keep it as a basis from which I can recover the rest, and rebuild everything else on the fly. That habit drew me to mathematics and physics, where a small set of ideas can account for an enormous amount.

At UC Santa Cruz I work with C. Seshadhri on the longest increasing subsequence problem, one of the dynamic programs I mentioned at the start. An equivalent way to ask it is how far a list is from sorted, meaning the fewest entries that must be deleted to leave it in increasing order. Michael Saks and Seshadhri showed that this distance can be estimated to within a multiplicative factor as close to 1 as we like, in time polylogarithmic in the length of the list, by reading only a tiny fraction of it. They then proposed the idea for a simpler algorithm with the same guarantee, built on a safety condition and a dichotomy argument, and I have taken the lead in turning that idea into a complete algorithm with a full proof. Several steps did not go through as originally envisioned, and finding new arguments for them has been a large part of the work.
