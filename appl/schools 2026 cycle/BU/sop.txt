Statement of purpose 
Many of the first algorithms we learn, such as those for longest increasing subsequence and edit distance, are dynamic programs: they fill in a table of answers to smaller subproblems until the final answer appears. This is already a striking trade, since it often takes a question whose search space is exponentially large and answers it in polynomial time. My current project asks when we can do even better: what can we conclude about a solution if we do not allow ourselves to fill in that whole table?
While now I am working in theoretical computer science, I didn't start that way. At UC Davis I earned a bachelor's degree in mathematics and physics with high honors and wrote an undergraduate thesis advised by Martin Fraas. That work became a paper in the Journal of Mathematical Physics, "Poincaré disk as a model of squeezed states of a harmonic oscillator," which describes a correspondence between squeezed states of a quantum harmonic oscillator and the geometric motions on the hyperbolic disk. He suggested that such a correspondence might exist; I proved it, and showed that it gives a way to visualize questions in control theory. That project taught me to look for the right geometric picture that makes a messy family of objects behave simply, and to carry a problem from a vague question to a finished proof. I now apply the same habit to algorithms.
At UC Santa Cruz I work with C. Seshadhri on the longest increasing subsequence problem, one of the dynamic programs I mentioned at the start. Solving it exactly means filling in a table, and the question is how much of that table we can avoid. Michael Saks and Seshadhri showed that a list's distance from being sorted, meaning the fewest entries that must be deleted to leave it increasing, can be estimated to within a multiplicative factor as close to 1 as we like, in time polylogarithmic in the length of the list. Their algorithm is powerful but intricate, and they later sketched a simpler one. It repeatedly cuts the list at a point where few entries are out of order across the cut, follows one piece at random, and estimates that piece with a cruder version of itself, so each round sharpens the estimate. I have taken the lead in turning that idea into a complete algorithm with a full proof. Several steps did not go through as originally envisioned, and finding new arguments for them has been a large part of my work. I also established a multiplicative bound and found an algorithm with a constant exponent independent of the accuracy parameter, the distance to monotonicity, and the length of the list. A manuscript describing these results is in preparation.
Because longest increasing subsequence is a dynamic program, this result points to a broader question, and that is how we arrived at our next step. Professor Seshadhri and I are now pursuing the question of what properties a dynamic programming problem needs so that it can be approximated top down, as the longest increasing subsequence algorithm is. My goal for the degree is to classify which dynamic programming problems can be approximated this way. A companion question is where the approach fails, since an answer to "why not" is a statement about the limits of approximation and so connects to complexity theory; I want to learn the complexity-theoretic tools needed to prove such statements. Beyond this project, I want my PhD to follow the question of how much work a problem really requires wherever it leads in algorithms and complexity, including randomized and sublinear algorithms.
I have presented the simplified algorithm at the UC Santa Cruz theory seminar, to an audience working across theory. This fall I am also hosting the seminar, which means finding speakers, introducing them, and making sure the talks are understandable to students who are new to the area.
I want to take this question to Boston University because several faculty there study it from different sides, and I am looking for a place where I can learn from more than one of them. I found theory by talking through problems with someone whose background differed from mine, and I would like a PhD that works the same way: an advisor to learn from closely, and nearby people who see the same question differently.
Krzysztof Onak's work sits closest to what I do now. He studies what an algorithm can still do when it can afford only a little memory or time, which is exactly the limit my longest increasing subsequence algorithm works under. Working with him would let me keep building on the problem I know best while seeing how the same ideas carry into settings I have not worked in yet.
Nathan Klein and Alina Ene design approximation algorithms, which aim for answers provably close to the best rather than exactly right. That is the spirit of my own project, and since my next step is to understand which dynamic programs can be approximated this way, I would value learning how they decide what makes a problem approachable. Klein's algorithms also use randomness, as mine do.
Andrea Lincoln asks why some problems seem to resist faster algorithms. Every positive result I hope to prove comes with the question of where the approach must fail, and I would want her perspective on how to turn a "why not" into a proof. Mark Bun studies what can be learned from limited or protected data, another version of drawing conclusions from only part of the information, and I would be glad to learn from him as well.
After getting my PhD I plan to continue my research and hope to one day become a professor. I hope to join a department where I can continue this work and follow the questions it raises into neighboring areas, and to keep learning how to explain what I find clearly.
